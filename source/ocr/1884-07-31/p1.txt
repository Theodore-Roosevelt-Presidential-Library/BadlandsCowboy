Starting on at 7 o'clock we reached

The morning of the 5th we started for
Deadwood in one of Mr. Spear's stages,
driven by Hank Monk, a fit descendant
of the original. We had the honor of a
share of Hank's seat and found him a
regular encyclopedia of information.
The first part of the ride was over the
same agricultural country for which the
Hills are famous. Many gypsum beds
were seen, practically valueless now,
but with railroad facilities it will be
come a valuable export, for nse as a fer
tilizer and to be manufactured into
plaster of paris. Falsebottom is a cu
rious freak of nature that we crossed
midway on our ride. It is the wide
channel of a river but contains not a
drop of water. A few miles above, there
is a strong current that sinks gradually
into the sand, continues its course un
derground and "bobs up serenely from
below" several miles farther down.
Soon we are in the Hills proper and.now
it is almost a continuous ascent to Dead
wood, eight miles distant. The road
bed is a remarkably good one, although
winding along at the foot and on the
sides of Hills from four to eight hundred
feet high. Everywhere, covering the
sides and summits of the Hills has
grown a dense forest of pine, closely re
sembling the Norway species. It is
rather harder, thus improving its build
ing properties. Except for the brick
buildings, every house in the Black Hills
region is built from these pine forests.
The deep red waters ot the streams at
tracted our attention. The discoloration
is caused by the pulverized quartz
hammered into powder by the stamps
of the immense Homestake mills and
others. The Homestake controls the
water right of the Hills virtually, con
ducting it for many miles over mount
ains and gulches, by a system of ditches
and flumes that is a triumph of engi
neering skill. This water is conducted
to other mills than thoee of the Home
stake system and thus a large royalty is
made by them.
