We have before had occasion to
allude to the weak arguments employed
by the Western Bural in its articles
against the range cattle industry. It
wasone of the leaders of the eastern
agricultural press a few months ago in
an endeavor to-compel range cattlemen
to lease the land... they occupied. As any
reasonable man should' have foreseen,
the selfish animus of the crusade was so
manifest that it fell of its own weight.
There were no arguments in right or
justice to support it. Now, however, the
Western Rural speaks of cowmen as "in
terlopers."'An interloper is one who
meddles in business in which he has no
concern. The only possible way in
which this term can apply to rangemen
is that their-product, by ooming into"
competition with stall-fed cattle, makes
them "interlopers." Graut that in this
sense they, are interlopers and what does
it prove? By the Rural's own words
they are interlopers because they com
pete in open market for business. An
argument then, based on such a' founda
tion, would totally destroy all business
principles. Competition is not only the
life of trade, but in this case, competi
tion is the greatest boon imaginable to
tho poorer classes of the United States.
There is no need of an array of figures
to prove that the range cattle industry
has decreased the price of meat so that
it is now a regular food for poor men,
where an advance of a few cents a pound
would put it in the list of luxuries, sel
dom or never to be tasted. Suppose that
the Kural's expressed wish should be re
alized and that "our farms produce all
the cattl* for which there is snch a de
mand." The.utter folly of such a state
ment and wish is so apparent as scarcely
to need explanation. If eastirn farmers
could supply the beet^denfend, why in
the world don't they do it? The Rural's
answer would he "they can't do it at the
cheap price of the range beef." They
couldn't possibly do it anyway, but take
the Rnral's answer as final and we haye
an astonishing boomerang of a social
science argument that an industry shonld
be annihilated for the benefit of a small
number of men. which, by its existence,
saves millions of dollars a year to con
sumers. But to prop up its argument,
the Snral would say. this business is
made possible because it is conducted on
land that belongs to the United States.

Wonld it be better to stop this food sup
ply, to the detriment of every man, wom
an and child in the country, because
rent is not paid for for the use of land
that no one else wants? The Rural's
arguments are too narrow apd selfish to
find many adherents. It argues for a
small class against all the rest of the
people, a position perfectly untenable
under a republican form of government.
In conclusion we would tell the Rural
that the very fact of its existence is
one of the strongest points
in its favor. No such abuse as the Rural
claims the range industry to be, could
keep its footing -in the United States.
It would long since have been annihilat
ed by legislative enactment.
