The Herd Law.

The future actions of the legislature
are rather hard to bank on, but of one
think there is a certainty. The writer
of this article has personally interviewed
a large nuinber of the. legislators, and to
a man, so far as interviewed, they were
in favor of a repeal W the herd law arid
the establishment of a fence law. Espe
cially are the Hills members in favor of
this and, as being the only place in Da
kota where the herd law,is abolished and
a fence law established, it will be well to
ex mine ite operation. The whole coun
try surrounding the Hills and up into
the foot-hills is first class agricultural
country. It is also a. good stock
country, and the coullict between agri
cultural and stock interests now liable
to happen at any time here, was there
solved long. ago. The herd law was
made inopeiative, and the fence law es
tablished. Under this law each farmer
must'enclose his ranch with a fence con
sisting of four boards, or-wires, securely
nailed to posts not over eight feet apart.
The law works perfectly, and from many
conversations-in the Hills we believe
that there is not a man thero who would
vote for the repeal of the fence law. Of
course.it brings more expense on the
farmor at the outset, but all agree that
the fences far more than pay for theui
pelves. They are an almost certain pro
tection against the depredations of stock,
ihsy immediately enhance the value of
the property enclosed more than the first
cost of the fence, in rnauy ways they en
courage the farmer in traits of economy
and neatness, and, especially to the one
accustomed

to the unbounded wheat

fields of the rest of Dakota, ty lend an
indescribable charm to the country that
can only be explained by the presence of
fences.

If this law is applicable to the Hills
country, how much more shonld it apply
to the Bad Lauds? This is pre-emi
nently a stock country, and not an ag
ricultural country. Tho Hills country i»
far better for farming than it "is for
Stock.

Then, under the present'herd law, it is
a perfectly simple thing tor any evil dis
posed person to start an 8x10 garden
patch, get cattle to run over it and cinch
the-owner of the stock many times the
amount of the damage. This is a se
rious drawback to tlie growth of the
stock industry, and something that con
cerns every citizen of Dakota, who is
looking toward Dakota's advancement.
The stock interests in her borders are
already large, but under proper laws
they will be vastly larger.

As far as we are concerned, we think
it would be altogether better in the end
to establish a fence law for the whole
West Missouri country. This however,
would entail hardship on many poor set
tles, although among those who can af
afford it, and view the matter from an
unbiased standpoint, admit that a fence
law ig better than a herd law.

There is certainly serious opposition in
many agricultural quarters against tlie
fence law, but this opposition in no way

lulls.
